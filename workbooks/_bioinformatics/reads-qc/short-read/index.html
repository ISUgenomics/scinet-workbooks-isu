This lesson assumes familiarity with basic sequencing concepts and the `FASTQ` format. If you need a refresher, start with [Sequencing technologies and assay types](/bioinformatics/resources/sequencing), [Reads, contigs, scaffolds, and nucleotide sequences](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature) and [File formats in Bioinformatics](/bioinformatics/resources/file_formats).
## Overview This lesson is a direct continuation of the introduction to [Sequencing Reads Quality Control](../), and focuses on how quality assessment and preprocessing decisions apply to **Illumina-style short reads**. It explains what short-read QC reports are measuring and what the most common warning patterns actually mean. The goal is to recognize significant read-level QC signals, understand when trimming and/or filtering is justified, and choose an appropriate preprocessing strategy before downstream analysis. {% include overviews %} ## Short reads Short reads are typically high-accuracy sequencing reads about **50-300 bases** long, most commonly generated by [Illumina platforms](/bioinformatics/resources/sequencing#sequencing-technologies) in [single-end or paired-end](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature#read-type-and-length) format. The limited read length brings direct QC implications: - base loss can remove a substantial fraction of the read, - short inserts can produce clear adapter read-through, - differences between read 1 and read 2 can directly affect how safely the data can be trimmed and retained for downstream analysis. Therefore, short-read quality control is not only about finding low-quality bases. It is also about recognizing the technical patterns that explain why a dataset may behave badly later during alignment, quantification, or interpretation. In practice, most read preprocessing decisions come down to a small set of questions: - **What problem is visible in the reads?** *For example: low-quality tails, adapter carryover, unusual GC content, or strong duplication.* - **How severe is it?** *Not every warning requires read trimming or filtering. Decide whether the problem affects downstream analysis enough to justify preprocessing.* - **What must be preserved in the read structure?** *Paired-end consistency, barcodes, UMIs, and assay-specific sequence context all matter. Decide which correction changes the data in a helpful rather than destructive way.* - **Which tool matches the problem most directly?** *Different tools are better for assessment, adapter-aware trimming, k-mer-based filtering, or standard paired-end cleanup.* ## What QC evaluates ### Base quality Per-base quality reflects the confidence of individual base calls across the read. In short-read datasets, quality often declines toward the 3' end, but the magnitude and shape of that decline matter more than the mere existence of a warning, because they help determine whether the affected sequence should be trimmed or can still be retained. For a refresher on [Phred quality scores](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature#quality-score) and how they are stored in `FASTQ`, see [Reads, contigs, scaffolds, and nucleotide sequences](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature#fastq-format). ### Adapter contamination Sequencing adapters are short synthetic sequences attached to DNA or cDNA fragments during library preparation so the fragments can bind to the flow cell and be sequenced. [Adapter contamination](/glossary) occurs when part of that synthetic sequence is still present in the reads. This can happen when the biological insert is shorter than the sequencing read, so the instrument reads through the fragment and into the adapter sequence, or when library preparation artifacts persist in the final data. Adapter contamination is one of the most common reasons to trim short reads before downstream analysis, especially when read-through occurs in short-insert libraries. ### Read length and layout Short-read datasets also need to be structurally consistent. A pipeline may expect reads in a specific [layout](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature#read-type-and-length), such as single-end or paired-end, and within a usable length range. Preprocessing steps that break pairing, create many unpaired reads, or over-shorten the surviving reads can make later stages less reliable. To revisit how sequencing reads differ by type and size, see [Reads, contigs, scaffolds, and nucleotide sequences](/bioinformatics/resources/nomenclature#read-type-and-length). ### Duplication and overrepresented sequence High duplication levels or overrepresented sequence patterns mean that many reads are identical or unusually frequent. This may indicate technical artifacts, biological redundancy, strong expression skew, or contamination. These signals should be interpreted in context rather than treated as automatic failures, especially in assays where highly abundant molecules are expected. ### GC distribution GC distribution describes how much of the read sequence is made up of guanine (`G`) and cytosine (`C`) bases. It helps reveal whether read composition is plausible for the assay and organism. Major deviations from the expected GC profile of the sample, or from other comparable samples in the same dataset, can indicate contamination, platform- or run-specific bias, library-preparation effects, or highly specialized sequence composition. ## Common QC patterns | Pattern in QC report | Likely cause | Likely consequence | First response | |----------------------|--------------|--------------------|----------------| | gradual 3' quality decline | expected short-read deterioration, run-cycle effects | lower confidence near read ends | inspect per-base quality and consider conservative quality trimming | | strong adapter signal near read ends | short inserts, adapter read-through, incomplete cleanup | spurious sequence content, poorer mapping | use adapter-aware trimming | | one mate is clearly worse than the other | asymmetric library behavior or instrument effects | unbalanced paired-end quality | inspect both mates separately before choosing parameters | | unusual GC distribution | contamination, strong bias, sample-specific composition | misleading interpretation, poor comparability | compare across samples and inspect overrepresented sequences | | many short surviving reads after trimming | aggressive filters or poor original quality | lower mappability, data loss | reconsider cutoff settings and minimum length | ## Preprocessing options Read preprocessing is used to correct or remove technical artifacts that would otherwise reduce the reliability of downstream alignment, quantification, or interpretation. The two main approaches are: - [trimming](#trimming), which **removes part of a read** while preserving the remaining sequence for downstream analysis; - [filtering](#filtering), which **removes entire reads** that do not meet quality, length, or content criteria.
Not all QC issues lead directly to preprocessing: [duplication and overrepresented sequences](#duplication-and-overrepresented-sequences) often require interpretation first, because they may reflect real biology, technical bias, or contamination depending on the dataset.
**Do not trim just to make every QC module pass.** The best preprocessing strategy is the one that removes meaningful technical artifacts while preserving the sequence information your downstream analysis still needs.
### Trimming #### Quality trimming Quality trimming removes low-confidence sequence, usually from read ends. It is most helpful when low-quality tails are systematic enough to interfere with downstream analysis but not so severe that the sample should simply be excluded. #### Adapter-aware trimming Adapter-aware trimming targets known sequencing adapter sequence rather than trimming by quality alone. This is the correct first response when the main problem is adapter carryover or insert read-through. #### Paired-end synchronization For paired-end data, trimming one mate can affect the interpretation and usability of the other. Some tools preserve synchronized pairs explicitly, while others require more careful handling of read structure and outputs. ### Filtering #### Contaminant filtering Some tools can match and remove specific unwanted sequence content beyond standard adapters. This is particularly useful when QC suggests contamination or recurring technical sequence that should not remain in the dataset. #### Length filtering Length filtering removes reads that become too short after trimming to remain informative. The key tradeoff is between retaining marginal reads and preserving enough sequence context for reliable downstream use. ## Choosing a tool | Tool | Best for | Strengths | Common misuse | |------|----------|-----------|---------------| | [`FastQC`](./qc_fastqc) | quality assessment | fast, interpretable, standard first check | users assume warnings automatically dictate trimming | | [`BBDUK`](./trim_bbduk) | adapter + contaminant filtering with quality trimming | flexible k-mer matching, good for combined filtering tasks | parameters are tuned too aggressively without validation | | [`Cutadapt`](./trim_cutadapt) | adapter-aware trimming with flexible read-structure handling | strong adapter logic, good for special cases such as UMI-aware scenarios | users trim assay-specific bases without checking read design | | [`fastp`](./trim_fastp) | routine short-read adapter trimming, quality filtering, and QC reporting | fast all-in-one workflow, widely used for standard Illumina cleanup | users accept default filtering without checking whether the trimmed output still fits the assay | | [`Trimmomatic (legacy)`](./trim_trimmomatic) | legacy standard paired-end trimming with explicit module order | transparent workflow, still common in teaching and older batch pipelines | users overlook unpaired outputs or overuse sliding-window trimming | ## Validate output reads Preprocessing decisions should be validated with a post-processing QC step. In short-read workflows, this usually means running [FastQC](./qc_fastqc) again on the trimmed and/or filtered reads and comparing the new report with the original QC results. This second QC pass is often more informative than the initial report, because it shows whether the chosen preprocessing actually addressed the original problem. ### QC validation checklist - **Did base quality improve in the problematic regions?** *For example, a poor-quality 3' tail should become shorter or less pronounced, while the rest of the read should remain usable.* - **Did adapter content decrease where expected?** *If adapter read-through was the original problem, the follow-up report should show a clear reduction rather than the same signal repeating after preprocessing.* - **Are read length and layout still appropriate?** *Check whether too many reads became too short, whether paired-end structure was preserved, and whether large numbers of reads were left unpaired.* - **Did duplication or overrepresented sequences become easier to interpret?** *These patterns do not always disappear after preprocessing, but the follow-up QC should help you tell whether the remaining signal is more likely biological, technical, or contaminant in origin.* - **Do the processed reads now better support the planned downstream analysis?** *The best outcome is not the fewest warnings, but a read set that is cleaner, still informative, and more suitable for alignment, quantification, or other downstream use.* ## If your case is...

Start with [Illumina short-read quality check with FastQC](./qc_fastqc). The goal is to determine whether the warnings reflect a real preprocessing need or simply a pattern that should be documented and monitored.
{% include accordion title="mild 3' quality drop in standard Illumina RNA-seq" controls="srqc1" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
This is a common and often manageable short-read pattern. Start by checking whether the decline is consistent across samples and whether it reaches clearly low-confidence territory. If trimming is justified, the tutorial [Standard adapter trimming, quality filtering, and QC reporting with fastp](./trim_fastp) is usually the most direct starting point. If you need to match an older pipeline that already uses sliding-window logic, use [Sliding-window quality trimming and length filtering with Trimmomatic (legacy)](./trim_trimmomatic).
{% include accordion title="quality decline without clear adapter contamination" controls="srqc1a" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
This usually points more strongly to **quality trimming** than to adapter-aware trimming. If the decline is limited to the read ends and the remaining read length stays useful, conservative trimming may help. If the drop is mild and downstream mapping is still expected to be reliable, documenting the pattern may be enough.
{% include accordion title="strong adapter contamination after short-insert sequencing" controls="srqc2" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
Adapter-aware trimming is the priority here. If the problem is primarily standard adapter carryover, use [Adapter and UMI-aware trimming with poly-G tail correction using Cutadapt](./trim_cutadapt). If the dataset may also need combined contaminant filtering, compare that workflow with [K-mer-based adapter and contaminant trimming/filtering with BBDUK](./trim_bbduk).
{% include accordion title="adapter-aware plus UMI-aware handling" controls="srqc3" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
Do not treat these reads as generic short reads until you verify where the UMI or barcode information lives. Use [Adapter and UMI-aware trimming with poly-G tail correction using Cutadapt](./trim_cutadapt) to trim adapter sequence while preserving important barcode or UMI structure in the reads.
{% include accordion title="suspected contaminant sequence in addition to adapters" controls="srqc4" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
When the issue goes beyond ordinary adapter trimming, a k-mer-based tool is often more appropriate. Start with [K-mer-based adapter and contaminant trimming/filtering with BBDUK](./trim_bbduk).
{% include accordion title="warnings remain even though the reads look mostly usable" controls="srqc4a" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
This is common, especially when the warnings reflect sequence composition, assay design, or mild technical bias rather than a correctable artifact. The goal is not to make every module pass, but to decide whether the remaining warnings are likely to interfere with downstream analysis.
{% include accordion title="one mate consistently looks worse than the other" controls="srqc5" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
Inspect both mates separately before deciding on a shared trimming strategy. Paired-end synchronization matters, and some tools handle this more explicitly than others. A good first step is [Illumina short-read quality check with FastQC](./qc_fastqc), followed by the trimming tutorial that best matches the actual problem.
{% include accordion title="many reads become too short after trimming or filtering" controls="srqc5a" expanded=false class="outline" icon=false %}
This often means the preprocessing settings are too aggressive for the original read length or insert size. Revisit quality thresholds, minimum-length cutoffs, and whether the original problem justified that level of sequence removal.
